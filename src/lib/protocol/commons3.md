# On "Process" vs "Commons"

## The Case for "Process"

**Process** has real appeal:

### **Theoretical Precision**
- It's literally what it is: a process with slots
- Avoids the ambiguity of "commons" (resource? governance structure? both?)
- Emphasizes the *dynamic* nature—not a thing, but an unfolding
- Maps cleanly to how developers think: "We're implementing process a3f8c9d2"

### **Neutrality**
- Less politically loaded than "commons"
- Easier adoption in contexts where "commons" might trigger ideological resistance
- "Process templates" sounds professional, legible to existing orgs

### **Compositional Clarity**
- "Process composed of sub-processes" is immediately intelligible
- "Commons composed of sub-commons" feels slightly awkward
- The nesting is more obvious: processes naturally contain processes

## The Case Against "Process"

But here's what you lose:

### **1. Political Clarity**

"Process" is **ideologically neutral in a bad way**. It hides what you're actually doing.

A capitalist firm has "processes." A Soviet bureau has "processes." A commons has "processes."

**The word doesn't declare what's different.**

When you say "We're building commons a3f8c9d2," you're making a claim:
- This is communal, not private
- This is shared, not owned
- This is for us, not for profit

When you say "We're building process a3f8c9d2," you're just... describing a workflow. Could be Amazon's warehouse optimization. Could be a DAO. Could be anything.

**The name should make the politics legible.**

### **2. The Double Meaning Is The Point**

Remember the beautiful ambiguity:
- "Coordination patterns are commons" (knowledge commons)
- "This infrastructure is a commons" (resource commons)

**Both are true. That's not confusion—that's synthesis.**

You're making coordination patterns into commons (content-addressed, unownable) AND you're structuring production as commons (communal, not output-based).

**"Process" captures only half of this.** It describes the coordination mechanism but says nothing about the property relations.

### **3. Historical Resonance**

"Commons" carries weight:
- Enclosure and resistance to enclosure
- Collective management of shared resources
- Centuries of struggle over who controls what

"Process" carries... what? Business process reengineering? Bureaucratic procedures?

**The historical baggage of "commons" is a feature, not a bug.** It situates what you're doing in a long arc of people fighting for communal control over production.

### **4. It Changes How People Relate to It**

Imagine two framings:

**"We need to optimize our process"**
→ Sounds like efficiency, KPIs, Lean Six Sigma
→ The process serves production
→ Instrumental, technical

**"We need to steward our commons"**
→ Sounds like collective responsibility, care, maintenance
→ We serve the commons (and it serves us)
→ Political, ethical

**Language shapes practice.** If you call it a "process," people will treat it like a tool. If you call it a "commons," people will treat it like a... well, like a commons. Something we collectively hold, govern, and reproduce.

## The Deeper Issue

Here's what I think is really at stake:

**"Process" lets you have the technical innovation without the political commitment.**

You could build this whole system, call it "process templates," and deploy it to:
- Optimize corporate supply chains
- Streamline government bureaucracy
- Make markets more efficient

**Nothing in the word "process" resists this.**

But if you call it "commons," you're making a claim: **This is for communal production. This is not for enclosure. This is infrastructure for a different political economy.**

Sure, someone could still use "commons templates" for capitalist purposes. You can't prevent that with naming.

But at least when they do, **the contradiction is visible**. "We're using commons to optimize profit extraction" is obviously perverse. "We're using processes to optimize profit extraction" is just... Tuesday.

## What Marx Would Say

I think Marx would say: **The name should express the social relation, not just the technical form.**

A commodity isn't just "a useful thing." It's a useful thing *produced under specific social relations* (wage labor, private property, exchange).

A commons isn't just "a coordinated process." It's a coordinated process *that presupposes communal production* (no outputs, public patterns, collective governance).

**"Process" names the form. "Commons" names the relation.**

And relations are what matter. The whole point of your critique is that capitalism and Soviet planning both had "processes" but preserved fundamentally extractive relations (output-based ownership, whether private or state).

**If you call it "process," you've left the relation unnamed.** The technical innovation is there, but the political transformation is invisible.

## My Recommendation

**Keep "Commons."**

Yes, it's ambiguous. Yes, it's loaded. Yes, it will make some people uncomfortable.

**That's the point.**

The ambiguity forces clarity: "Wait, is this a thing or a pattern?" → "Both. That's the synthesis."

The political charge filters adoption: "This is for communal production, you in or not?"

The historical weight anchors it: "We're continuing the long struggle against enclosure."

**The name should make your politics legible and your technical innovation inseparable from those politics.**

"Process" lets you have the tech without the politics.
"Commons" makes the politics constitutive of the tech itself.

---

If you want to hedge, you could use both:

**"Commons Process"** or **"Process Commons"**

But honestly? I think that dilutes both. 

**Just "Commons."** 

Let the code speak: these are templates for communal production, they're mathematically unownable, they compose into larger commons, they presuppose social labor.

That's a commons. Call it what it is.
